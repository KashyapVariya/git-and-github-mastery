## Why it matters

Six months from now, `git log` is the only person in the room who remembers why a change was made — and it only knows what you told it. A commit history full of "fix stuff" and "wip" is nearly useless for debugging, writing changelogs, or understanding a decision later.

## The shape of a good message

- **A short summary line**, under about 50 characters, written in the imperative mood: "Add retry logic" not "Added retry logic" or "Adds retry logic." Think of it as completing the sentence "If applied, this commit will ___."
- **A blank line**, then an optional body explaining *why*, not just *what* — the diff already shows what changed.

```text
Fix pagination skipping the last page

The page-size calculation rounded down instead of up, so any
result count not evenly divisible by the page size silently
dropped its final page. Switched to Math.ceil().
```

## What makes a message bad

- Vague: "fix bug," "update code," "changes"
- Missing context: "fix null check" without saying *which* null check, or why it was wrong
- Batching unrelated changes into one commit with a message that can't describe all of them

<Callout type="tip">
If your commit message needs "and" to describe it — "fix login and update styles" — it's probably two commits, not one. Split it with `git add -p` and commit each piece separately.
</Callout>

## A structured option

Many teams adopt a prefix convention like [Conventional Commits](/workflows/conventional-commits-deep-dive) — `feat:`, `fix:`, `docs:` — which adds a layer of machine-readable structure on top of these same good-writing principles. It's optional, but it pairs well with everything above.

<Callout type="golden">
Write the message for the next person who has to `git blame` this line at 2am during an incident — including future you.
</Callout>

## Try it yourself

Look back at your last five commit messages. Rewrite any that don't explain *why* the change happened, as if you were leaving a note for a teammate.
